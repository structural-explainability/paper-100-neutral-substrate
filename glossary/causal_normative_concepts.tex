\subsection*{Causal and Normative Concepts}
\textit{Terms relating to the classes of commitment excluded from neutral substrates.}

%-----------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection*{Causal Concepts}
%-----------------------------------------------------

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5cm]

\item[Causal Claim]
A claim that asserts or denies a causal relation
among entities, occurrences, events, conditions, or processes.
Examples include claims that one event caused another,
that an omission prevented an outcome,
or that a condition contributed to an occurrence.
In a neutral substrate, causal claims may be represented
as reified, attributed, provenance-bearing content,
but they may not be asserted as substrate-layer facts.

\item[Causal Predicate]
A predicate symbol used to express causal relations,
causal contribution, prevention, enablement, production,
explanation, or dependence.
The syntactic occurrence of a causal predicate
is not itself prohibited by neutrality.
What neutrality prohibits is the substrate-layer assertion
of causal commitments using such predicates.
Causal predicates may occur as the content
of reified assertions.

\item[Causal Attribution]
The assignment of a causal relation between entities,
occurrences, events, or conditions by an interpretive framework.
Causal attribution is typically framework-dependent
because different causal models may attribute influence,
responsibility, contribution, or explanation differently.
In a neutral substrate, causal attributions belong
to interpretive layers or to reified attributed content,
not to substrate-layer facts.
Contrast with \emph{Causal Commitment}.

\item[Causal Commitment]
A substrate-layer assertion that treats a causal relation
as true independently of any particular interpretive framework.
Examples include asserting that one occurrence caused another,
that an action causally contributed to an outcome,
or that a condition explains an event.
Under the neutrality theorem, the substrate may not make causal
commitments when their truth is contestable across admissible frameworks.

\item[Pre-Causal]
The property of a substrate whose foundational layer
does not assert causal conclusions as substrate-layer facts.
Pre-causal does not mean acausal, causation-denying,
or unable to represent causal discourse.
It means prior to causal attribution:
the substrate represents entities, occurrences,
provenance, temporal order, participation,
and other framework-invariant structure
without asserting causal conclusions as true.
Causal reasoning may occur in interpretive layers,
where model assumptions are explicit
and disagreement remains representable.
Causation is externalized, not eliminated.

\end{description}

%-----------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection*{Normative and Deontic Concepts}
%-----------------------------------------------------

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5cm]

\item[Normative Claim]
A claim that asserts or denies an evaluative,
legal, moral, institutional, policy-based,
or responsibility-bearing conclusion.
Examples include claims that an action was permitted,
prohibited, obligatory, compliant, noncompliant,
justified, violative, blameworthy, liable, or responsible.
In a neutral substrate, normative claims may be represented
as reified, attributed, provenance-bearing content,
but they may not be asserted as substrate-layer facts.

\item[Normative Predicate]
A predicate symbol used to express evaluation,
obligation, permission, prohibition, compliance,
violation, justification, responsibility, liability,
or related normative status.
The syntactic occurrence of a normative predicate
is not itself prohibited by neutrality.
What neutrality prohibits is the substrate-layer assertion
of normative commitments using such predicates.
Normative predicates may occur as the content
of reified assertions.

\item[Normative Attribution]
The assignment of normative status to an entity,
occurrence, action, omission, record, or institutional artifact
by an interpretive framework.
Normative attribution includes assigning obligations,
permissions, prohibitions, violations, compliance status,
responsibility, blame, liability, or justification.
In a neutral substrate, normative attributions belong
to interpretive layers or to reified attributed content,
not to substrate-layer facts.
Contrast with \emph{Normative Commitment}.

\item[Normative Commitment]
A substrate-layer assertion that treats an evaluative,
legal, moral, institutional, policy-based,
or responsibility-bearing conclusion as true
independently of any particular interpretive framework.
Normative commitments may reflect ethical, legal,
institutional, procedural, regulatory, or policy frameworks
and may therefore vary across admissible interpretive perspectives.
Under the neutrality theorem, the substrate may not make normative
commitments when their truth is contestable across admissible frameworks.

\item[Deontic Claim]
A normative claim specifically concerning obligation,
permission, prohibition, duty, authorization,
or requirement.
Deontic claims state what must, may, or must not be done
within some normative framework.
In a neutral substrate, deontic claims may be represented
as reified, attributed, provenance-bearing content,
but they may not be asserted as substrate-layer facts.

\item[Deontic Predicate]
A predicate symbol used to express obligation,
permission, prohibition, duty, authorization,
or requirement.
Deontic predicates are normative in character.
Their occurrence as reified content is compatible
with neutrality.
Their use in substrate-layer assertions of deontic commitments
is not compatible with neutrality.

\item[Deontic Commitment]
A substrate-layer assertion that treats an obligation, permission,
prohibition, duty, authorization, or requirement as holding
independently of any particular interpretive framework.
Deontic commitments are a subset of normative commitments:
they encode what ought, may, or must not be done,
rather than merely what exists, occurs, or is referenced.
Under the neutrality theorem, the substrate may not make deontic
commitments when their truth is contestable across admissible frameworks.

\item[Pre-Normative]
The property of a substrate whose foundational layer
does not assert normative conclusions as substrate-layer facts.
Pre-normative does not mean non-normative,
norm-denying, value-free, or unable to represent normative discourse.
It means prior to normative attribution:
the substrate represents entities, occurrences,
provenance, temporal order, participation,
institutional artifacts, and other framework-invariant structure
without asserting normative conclusions as true.
Normative reasoning may occur in interpretive layers,
where framework assumptions are explicit
and disagreement remains representable.
Normativity is externalized, not eliminated.

\end{description}

%-----------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection*{Framework Dependence and Externalization}
%-----------------------------------------------------

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5cm]

\item[Framework-Dependent]
A property of claims, conclusions, or attributions
whose truth or applicability may vary across
admissible interpretive frameworks.
Causal and normative claims are treated in this paper
as framework-dependent under the contestability assumption.
Framework-dependent claims may be represented by the substrate,
but they may not be asserted as substrate-layer facts
when neutrality is required.

\item[Assertion-Level Exclusion]
The rule that neutrality restricts what the substrate asserts
as true, not which words or predicate symbols may occur
in represented content.
Causal and normative claims may appear in the substrate
as reified, attributed, provenance-bearing assertions.
They may not be asserted of substrate individuals
as substrate-layer facts.

\item[Representation Without Endorsement]
The representation of a claim without asserting that claim
as true at the substrate layer.
A substrate may record that a framework, agent,
document, institution, or record asserted a causal
or normative claim.
Such representation preserves attribution and provenance
while leaving truth-evaluation to the relevant interpretive framework.

\item[Externalized Interpretation]
The placement of causal, normative, evaluative,
legal, policy-based, or responsibility-bearing conclusions
in interpretive layers rather than in the substrate layer.
Externalized interpretation allows multiple admissible frameworks
to derive incompatible conclusions over shared referents
without forcing the substrate to choose among them.

\end{description}
