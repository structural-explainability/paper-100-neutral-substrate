\section{Introduction: Accountability, Disagreement, and Neutral Substrates}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern data systems must support stable shared reference
across persistent legal, political, analytic, causal, and normative disagreement.
Legal interpretations diverge across jurisdictions and
over time~\citep{dworkin1986law,hart1961concept,berman2007pluralism};
political actors contest responsibility
and intent~\citep{bovens2007accountability,hood2011blame};
analytic frameworks disagree about
causation and attribution~\citep{woodward2003making}.
These disagreements are not anomalies to be resolved prior to data representation.
They are structural features of institutional environments
that data systems must accommodate
while maintaining stable reference to shared
entities, occurrences, and institutional artifacts.

Accountability is one motivating setting in which this requirement
becomes especially visible.
Accountability requires more than the collection of records or
the publication of datasets~\citep{fox2007transparency,bovens2014oxford}.
It requires the ability to refer stably to entities,
occurrences, and institutional artifacts~\citep{gruber1993translation,smith2010ontology}
while allowing competing explanations, evaluations, and judgments to coexist.
A system that collapses disagreement by embedding a single
causal or normative interpretation into its foundational
representation restricts accountability in settings characterized
by persistent interpretive disagreement,
because it forecloses contestation, comparison, and
framework-relative evaluation~\citep{bowker1999sorting,winner1980artifacts}.

This paper adopts a different framing of the design challenge.
Rather than enforcing interpretive agreement,
or treating disagreement primarily as semantic heterogeneity
to be resolved through alignment,
the goal is to identify the structural constraints
under which an ontology can function as a neutral substrate:
a shared representational base that maintains stable reference
across incompatible interpretive frameworks.

\subsection*{Design Problems in Shared Representation}

Ontology design has traditionally addressed two related but distinct
representation problems.
First, systems may assume \emph{shared interpretation}.
In this setting, participants agree on a common ontology that
provides a unified interpretation of shared representations.
Second, systems may face \emph{semantic heterogeneity}.
Here multiple ontologies exist, but disagreement is treated as
a translation problem addressed through alignment,
mapping, or mediation mechanisms.

The problem considered in this paper is different.
Many institutional and accountability-oriented environments exhibit
\emph{persistent interpretive disagreement}.
An \emph{interpretive framework} is a coherent set of rules,
principles, assumptions, methods, or norms used to evaluate,
explain, classify, or assign meaning to shared referents.
Legal, political, causal, analytic, and normative frameworks
are examples of interpretive frameworks in this sense.
Such frameworks may reach incompatible conclusions about the same
entities, occurrences, or institutional artifacts while continuing
to operate over a shared representational base.

Traditional ontology frameworks regulate
how agreement is structured across systems.
Neutral substrate architectures instead regulate
how disagreement can coexist without forcing
revision of the shared representational base.

This paper examines the implications
of this requirement for ontology design.
Specifically, it addresses the conditions under which an ontology
can function as a \emph{neutral substrate} for accountability:
a shared representational layer that supports stable reference to
entities, occurrences, and institutional artifacts while allowing
multiple incompatible interpretive frameworks to coexist.
Under this conception of neutrality, the substrate must remain usable
across divergent interpretive frameworks without requiring revision
of the substrate itself.
A neutral substrate, in this sense, is a foundational representation
that supports such coexistence without embedding the causal or
normative conclusions on which those frameworks may disagree.

In this paper, a \emph{substrate ontology} is an ontology that serves
as the shared representational base across interpretive frameworks.
Its foundational layer provides entities, occurrences,
institutional artifacts, provenance structures, and the stable
referential structure over which interpretive frameworks reason
and derive conclusions.
Section~\ref{sec:requirements} formalizes this stable referential
structure as a \emph{referential regime}: the substrate-level basis
for individuating referents, determining co-reference, and tracking
persistence across time, records, jurisdictions, or interpretive contexts.
For brevity, this ontology may be referred to as the \emph{substrate}.

This requires a careful selection of the
Level of Abstraction (LoA)~\citep{floridi2008levels}
at which the substrate operates.
Following Floridi, an LoA is defined by the set of
observables it makes available for representation.
In an ontological substrate, these observables correspond
to the predicate symbols available in the foundational vocabulary.
For a substrate to remain neutral,
its foundational assertions must provide stable shared reference
without settling the causal or normative questions
on which admissible interpretive frameworks may disagree.
Section~\ref{sec:requirements} states this assertion-level constraint
formally.

Conceptually, the architecture considered here separates three roles.
The substrate provides shared referents and framework-invariant
referential structure.
Interpretive frameworks reason over that structure using their own
rules, assumptions, methods, or norms.
The conclusions produced by those frameworks may disagree without
requiring revision of the substrate.
Section~\ref{sec:requirements} formalizes these roles.

The key design question is therefore what the substrate may assert
while remaining usable across incompatible interpretive frameworks.

This form of neutrality imposes strict design constraints.
Causal and normative conclusions may be derived by interpretive
frameworks, and causal or normative claims may be represented
as attributed content.
The substrate itself, however, must not settle those conclusions
as its own.
The formal requirements that make this constraint precise
are introduced in Section~\ref{sec:requirements}.

This paper adopts the following design constraint.
Under persistent disagreement, extensions to the substrate may
introduce incompatible causal accounts or normative attributions,
and those extensions are not required to converge.
Substrate revision is treated as an impermissible
mechanism for resolving interpretive conflict: the substrate
must remain stable across extensions whose conclusions are
mutually inconsistent.
This is a deliberate design choice, not a universal requirement.
Systems that can enforce interpretive convergence through
coordinated governance or shared authority do not face this
constraint; the results of this paper apply only to substrates
explicitly designed to remain stable without such coordination.

The contribution of this paper is not the observation that
interpretive frameworks may disagree about causal or normative
conclusions.
That fact is widely recognized across legal theory,
political analysis, and causal modeling.
The contribution is to show that,
in settings where substrate revision is treated as
an impermissible mechanism for resolving interpretive conflict,
this disagreement imposes
structural constraints on ontology design,
and to establish those constraints formally.

The central result is a biconditional:
under the stated contestability assumption and referential-invariance
scope condition, a substrate ontology satisfies neutrality if and only if
its foundational layer is restricted to framework-invariant referential
structure and does not assert causal or normative conclusions as
substrate-layer facts.

Neutrality is therefore possible,
under the stated assumptions and scope conditions,
if and only if the substrate is pre-causal and pre-normative.
Pre-causal and pre-normative do not mean acausal,
norm-denying, or unable to represent causal or normative discourse.
They mean that the substrate makes no causal or normative commitments.
Such propositions may be represented as reified, attributed,
provenance-bearing content or introduced within interpretive frameworks
layered above the substrate.
The precise conditions under which extensions may be introduced
are stated in Section~\ref{sec:requirements}.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work in ontology design,
legal informatics, and causal modeling, situating the present contribution.
Section~\ref{sec:requirements} defines the notion of a neutral substrate,
the referential-invariance scope condition, and the two neutrality
requirements.
Section~\ref{sec:formal-dev} establishes the central result:
a neutral substrate satisfies both requirements if and only if
its foundational layer is restricted to framework-invariant
referential structure and
does not assert causal or normative conclusions as substrate-layer facts.
Section~\ref{sec:implications} discusses the implications of this result for ontology design,
clarifying the substrate-level exclusions and requirements,
and what may be externalized to interpretive frameworks.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarizes the resulting constraints
and discusses their relevance for ontology design in accountability contexts.
