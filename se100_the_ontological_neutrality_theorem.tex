\begin{abstract}
  Modern data systems must support stable shared reference
  across persistent interpretive disagreement.
  This requirement imposes structural constraints
  on any ontology intended to function as a neutral substrate.
  This paper establishes a necessary and sufficient condition
  for ontological neutrality under an explicitly stated
  contestability assumption and scope condition.
  The central result is a biconditional:
  a substrate ontology satisfies neutrality if and only if its foundational
  layer is restricted to framework-invariant referential structure and does
  not assert causal or normative conclusions as substrate-layer facts.
  Neutrality here is the conjunction of two requirements:
  interpretive non-commitment and extension stability.
  The result holds under a contestability assumption for causal and
  normative propositions, and
  under the scope condition
  that the substrate's framework-invariant referential structure,
  including its referential regime, is fixed at the substrate layer
  and is not itself contested
  across the admissible frameworks under consideration.
  A referential regime consists of the individuation, co-reference, and
  persistence conditions by which entities, occurrences, and institutional
  artifacts are fixed and tracked across time, records, jurisdictions, or
  interpretive contexts.
  The result does not require causal or normative vocabulary to be absent
  from the substrate.
  Rather, it requires that causal and normative claims be represented, if at all,
  as reified, attributed, provenance-bearing assertions whose truth is evaluated
  within interpretive frameworks rather than asserted by the substrate itself.
  The paper does not propose a specific ontology or protocol.
  It identifies the structural constraints that any ontology must satisfy
  to function as a neutral substrate across admissible interpretive
  frameworks under persistent disagreement.
\end{abstract}

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Preview of Main Result.}
\emph{Let $\mathcal{S}$ be a substrate ontology intended to function
  as a neutral substrate across admissible interpretive frameworks,
  and let $\mathbb{F}$ be the set of all admissible frameworks.
  Suppose the domain satisfies the contestability assumption for causal
  and normative propositions and the scope condition that the
  framework-invariant referential structure asserted by $\mathcal{S}$,
including its referential regime, is fixed at the substrate layer
and is not itself contested
across the admissible frameworks under consideration.
  Then $\mathcal{S}$ satisfies the neutrality requirements
  of interpretive non-commitment and extension stability
  if and only if its foundational layer is restricted to
  framework-invariant referential structure and does not assert causal
  or normative conclusions as substrate-layer facts.}

\medskip
\noindent
\emph{Informally.}
The result applies to systems that adopt the design objective
of maintaining a neutral substrate under persistent interpretive disagreement.
Under this objective, the theorem establishes that a neutral substrate must be
pre-causal and pre-normative at the level of assertion.
This does not mean that causal or normative concepts are
absent, unimportant, or unreal.
It means that causal and normative conclusions may not be asserted
as substrate-layer facts.
They may instead be represented as reified, attributed,
provenance-bearing claims whose truth is evaluated within
interpretive frameworks layered above the substrate.

\medskip
\noindent
The substrate may assert framework-invariant referential structure:
entities, occurrences, institutional artifacts, provenance,
the referential regimes by which relevant referents are individuated,
co-referred, and tracked across time or context,
and other structural relations that assert neither causal efficacy
nor normative force.
If admissible frameworks disagree about any part of that referential
structure, including the regimes, then neutrality in the
sense defined here is unavailable until that contestation is resolved
or scoped out.
Systems that enforce interpretive convergence through governance or shared
authority, or that do not adopt the neutral-substrate design objective,
are not subject to these constraints.
