# Normative and Descriptive Distinction

Causal.
A relation in which one event, state, or process produces or brings about another.

Normative.
Pertaining to standards governing what ought to be done, believed, or valued.

Partition within Normative:

Deontic.
Norms governing obligation, permission, and prohibition.

Evaluative.
Norms governing judgments of value, such as good or preferable.

Epistemic.
Norms governing standards of belief, knowledge, and justification.

## Causal (Extended)

A relation in which one event, state, or process produces, brings about, or contributes to the occurrence of another.
Causal relations describe how states of the world generate other states of the world.
They belong to the descriptive domain and concern what does happen, not what ought to happen.

Example uses in ontology:

event causation
physical processes
system dynamics

In a neutral substrate context, causal relations are interpretive commitments that may be imposed by a framework but are not encoded in the substrate itself.

## Normative (Extended)

Relating to standards governing evaluation, correctness, or what ought to occur.
Normative structures determine whether actions, states, or beliefs conform to rules, obligations, permissions, or evaluative standards.

Normativity concerns standards and prescriptions, rather than causal production.

Normative structures are typically institutional or framework-dependent rather than intrinsic features of physical systems.

Within normative systems, several distinct subdomains are recognized.

### Partition Within Normative

#### Deontic

The branch of normativity concerned with obligations, permissions, and prohibitions.

Deontic structures specify what agents are required, allowed, or forbidden to do under a system of rules.

Common formal elements:

- Obligation – an action that must be performed
- Permission – an action that is allowed
- Prohibition – an action that is forbidden

Deontic logic is widely used in legal, institutional, and policy modeling.

#### Evaluative

Normativity concerning judgments of value or quality, such as good, bad, better, or preferable.

Evaluative norms guide assessment rather than obligation.

Examples:

- quality standards
- ethical evaluations
- aesthetic judgments

#### Epistemic

Normativity concerning standards for belief, knowledge, and justification.

Epistemic norms govern how beliefs ought to be formed or maintained, such as rules of evidence or rational inference.

Examples:

- evidential justification
- standards of proof
- rational belief revision
